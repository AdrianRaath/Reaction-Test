## Does caffeine improve reaction time?

Yes, and the evidence is deep. Caffeine blocks adenosine, the molecule that accumulates through the day and registers as tiredness, so alertness rises and responses come sooner. Across decades of studies, doses between roughly 40 and 300 mg improve reaction time, vigilance, and attention.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> The benefit is largest when you are tired, but a rested brain still gains.

<CaffeineZones />

The honest caveat: the size of the effect is tens of milliseconds at best, and it varies from person to person. Caffeine is one lever among several, and the cheapest gains sit elsewhere; our guide on <a href="/guides/how-to-improve-reaction-time">how to improve reaction time</a> ranks them. To find your own response, run a session on the <a href="/">reaction time test</a> before your next coffee and another an hour after. The history keeps both numbers.

## The dose is smaller than you think

A 2024 trial put first-person-shooter players through aim and reaction tasks at two doses: 1 mg per kilogram of body weight, about 83 mg or one strong cup of coffee, and 3 mg per kilogram, about 250 mg. Both doses beat water on shooting accuracy and reaction time. Neither beat the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

That result should change how you dose. Tripling the caffeine bought zero extra speed and raised the cost side of the ledger: heavy doses bring jitters, a racing pulse, and anxiety, which tax exactly the fine motor control and consistency a reaction task runs on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> One cup, taken at the right time, is the whole play.

## Timing the dose

Caffeine is nearly fully absorbed within 45 minutes, and blood levels peak anywhere from 15 minutes to two hours after drinking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> The esports trial saw its strongest effects 60 to 100 minutes after the dose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

The practical version: a coffee finished 45 minutes to an hour before you play puts the peak inside your session. A coffee gulped as you sit down peaks after the match. And a dose taken to rescue a session already going badly arrives too late to rescue anything.

## Caffeine borrows from tomorrow

Every dose has a second half-life you pay at night. Caffeine clears with a half-life around five hours, so a mid-afternoon coffee is still half-active at midnight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> Drake and colleagues measured what that does to sleep: a 400 mg dose significantly disrupted the night whether taken at bedtime, three hours before, or six hours before. The six-hour dose cut more than an hour of measured sleep, and the sleepers did not notice the loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup>

<CaffeineSleepCost />

That unnoticed hour matters more than the boost did. Sleep loss slows reaction time in ways caffeine can mask but cannot undo, so a late dose trades a small gain today for a measurable deficit tomorrow. Our guide on <a href="/guides/how-sleep-affects-reaction-time">how sleep affects reaction time</a> covers what that deficit looks like; the short version is that it compounds.

## If you use it every day, it works less

The awkward finding in the caffeine literature: with daily use, near-complete tolerance develops to caffeine's alerting effects. When researchers compare regular consumers dosed with caffeine against non-consumers given placebo, the caffeine drinkers perform no better. Much of what a daily drinker feels from the morning cup is relief of overnight withdrawal, a return to baseline rather than a rise above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup>

You do not have to quit coffee over this. The practical reading is about efficiency: the lower your everyday intake, the more an occasional, deliberately timed dose does when it counts. A player who saves caffeine for sessions that matter gets the 40 to 300 mg benefit; a player on four daily cups is mostly treating withdrawal.
