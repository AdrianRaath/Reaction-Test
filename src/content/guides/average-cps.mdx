## The average CPS, from the research

The closest science to a CPS test is the finger-tapping test, a standard clinical measure of motor speed. You tap a key with your index finger as fast as you can for 10 seconds, the same length as a Kohi click test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup>

In a norms study of 183 Spanish-speaking adults aged 19 to 60 in Arizona and California, the dominant hand averaged 49.5 taps per 10 seconds, or 4.9 a second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> A German population study of 610 right-handed adults, tapping on a force sensor, found 5.0 to 5.3 taps a second depending on age. Its authors placed those figures inside the 4.8 to 5.7 range that earlier studies report.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

<TapSurfaces />

A keyboard reads faster. In a Korean study of 72 adults, the dominant hand averaged 7.2 taps a second on a desktop keyboard and 7.1 on a tablet's on-screen keyboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> The three studies tested different people, so the chart can't pin the whole gap on the device. It does bracket the answer: about 5 a second on a clinical key, about 7 on a keyboard. The keyboard result is also the nearest published benchmark for the <a href="/spacebar-speed-test">spacebar speed test</a>, though the study doesn't name the key its participants pressed.

Mouse clicks have no published average. We found no study that measures clicks per second on a mouse in a general sample, so the 6 to 7 CPS averages quoted around the web are plausible but unmeasured. To get your own number, run the <a href="/kohi-click-test">Kohi click test</a>, which lasts the same 10 seconds as the lab trials, and set your score against the chart.

## Why longer tests score lower

Your fastest taps come first. In a lab study of index-finger movement at maximum speed, the rate started falling 7 to 9 seconds in and finished the 20-second trial at 73% of its starting pace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup> The researchers found no sign of tired muscles. They traced the slowdown to the nervous system: the muscles that bend and straighten the finger stopped taking turns and began firing together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup>

Researchers who ran longer trials found the same fade. Across 370 adults tapping for 30 seconds, speed fell about 17% from the fastest stretch to the slowest, and 18-to-30-year-olds lost as much as 50-to-70-year-olds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> In another lab, 11 men with a baseline rate of 7.1 taps a second finished each 30-second trial about 18% slower than they started.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup> Over three minutes the rate dropped about 40%, most of it inside the first minute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-7">7</a></sup>

Compare your own scores against runs of the same length. A 5-second test catches you before the slowdown begins, and a 10-second test ends a few seconds after it starts. Sixty seconds runs deep into it, so expect your 60-second CPS to sit well below your 5-second one. Try both on the <a href="/cps">CPS test</a> and watch the gap open.

## Hand, finger, and age

<TappingHands />

Your dominant hand taps about 10% faster than your other hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup> In the German study, switching hands cost more speed than two decades of age. Right-handers aged 37 to 50 tapped 5.34 times a second with the right hand and 4.91 with the left, and the right hand managed 4.97 in the 60 to 70 group.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

Finger choice matters as well. Your index finger outpaces your middle finger in lab comparisons,<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">5</a>,<a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> and on a standard grip the middle finger does your right-clicking. Expect a lower score on the <a href="/right-click-cps-test">right click CPS test</a> than on the left-button tests, and track it against its own history.

Age and sex move the averages by fractions of a tap per second. In the 370-person study, 18-to-30-year-olds outpaced 50-to-70-year-olds with the index and middle fingers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> Men averaged 5.3 taps a second on the clinical key against 4.7 for women,<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> and 5.40 against 4.95 on the force sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup> The spread inside each group is wider than the gap between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

## Jitter and butterfly clicking: what the research supports

Butterfly clicking alternates two fingers on one button. In a lab comparison of 11 men, alternating the index and middle fingers tapped 50% faster than one finger alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> Apply that to a one-finger rate of 5 to 6 a second and you land between 7.5 and 9. Claims of 15 to 25 CPS go far past that, and we found no published measurement behind them.

Jitter clicking tenses the forearm so the finger shakes against the button. We found no study that measures it. Most tapping research assumes a one-hand limit of 5 to 7 taps a second, and the fastest one-hand rate we found, 10 a second, came from a champion drummer holding a stick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> The researchers credited a loose wrist over a stiff one, the opposite of the tensed arm jitter clicking relies on.

Both techniques change the movement. The <a href="/cps">CPS test</a> counts every click it registers, so keep one technique across any runs you plan to compare.

## What counts as a good CPS

<CpsBands />

ReflexLab grades the CPS, Kohi, and right click tests on one scale. The one-finger lab averages fall in Intermediate, and the keyboard result falls in Advanced. Beat 7.2 over 10 seconds with one finger and you outpace the fastest average in the first chart. Pro starts at 8, past the 5 to 7 taps a second that most tapping studies treat as the one-hand limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup>

The bands grade every test length on the same scale, so a Pro score held for 60 seconds is a bigger feat than the same score over 5. Pick one length on the <a href="/cps">CPS test</a>, run it for a week, and chase your average instead of a single lucky run.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the average CPS?

Lab studies put one-finger tapping at about 5 taps per second, with most results between 4.8 and 5.7, and one study measured 7.2 on a computer keyboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a>,<a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> We found no published study of mouse clicks per second in a general sample, so the 6 to 7 CPS figures quoted online are plausible but unmeasured. Your speed also falls the longer you click, so an average needs a fixed test length to mean anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup>

### What is a good CPS for 10 seconds?

In a 10-second test, 7 CPS or more lands in ReflexLab's Advanced band, above the one-finger lab averages near 5. Beat 7.2 and you outpace the average for tapping on a computer keyboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> Scores of 8 and up pass the 5 to 7 taps a second that most tapping studies treat as the one-hand limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup>

### How fast can a human click?

Most tapping studies assume a one-hand limit of 5 to 7 taps per second,<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> and 11 men in one lab study had a baseline rate of 7.1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup> Alternating two fingers, the idea behind butterfly clicking, ran 50% faster than one finger in a lab comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> The fastest one-hand tapping we found in the research was 10 per second, by a champion drummer with a stick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup>
