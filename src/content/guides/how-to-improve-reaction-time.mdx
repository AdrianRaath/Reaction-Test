## What counts as a fast reaction time?

In laboratory conditions, healthy young adults average around 218 ms on a simple visual reaction test, and close to 200 ms once researchers subtract the delay added by their own equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> Browser tests read higher. The screen has to draw the color change, and your mouse and browser add their own delay before a click registers, so typical online medians sit around 273 ms.

A single attempt tells you little. Scores swing with alertness from one attempt to the next, and everyone anticipates or hesitates now and then. Your average over a full session is the number worth tracking, and it is the number the <a href="/">reaction time test</a> records for you.

Before you change anything in your routine, run one session to set a baseline. Every method below becomes measurable once you have that number.

## Can you get faster?

Within limits, yes. Genetics and age set your floor, and no training plan turns an average adult into a 150 ms outlier. The margin above that floor is real, though. People who play fast-paced action games respond faster than non-players on lab tasks that have nothing to do with games, and they lose no accuracy doing it. Training studies point the causal arrow the same way: assign non-gamers to action games for a few weeks and their response speed improves on tasks they never practiced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

Practice on the specific task works faster still. In one study, three days of practice on a simple response task shortened reaction times in 14 of 15 participants, with measurable changes in how their brains processed the incoming signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup>

Two kinds of gains run through the rest of this guide. Practice and exercise train you faster. Sleep, caffeine, and hardware stop slowing you down.

## 1. Practice with feedback

Reacting to a visual cue is a skill, and it sharpens with repetition. Your brain streamlines the specific path from seeing the change to firing the click, which is why the first gains arrive within days.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup>

Keep sessions short. Two to five minutes of full-attention attempts beats a long grind, because your attention degrades before your hand does. Stop when your times start drifting upward.

Feedback turns repetition into training. When you can see your session average, your personal best, and your trend across days, you learn which changes help and which are noise. Test after a bad night, then after a good one, and the sleep numbers below stop being abstract. The <a href="/">test</a> keeps that history for you, so the comparison costs nothing.

## 2. Sleep: the biggest lever

Dawson and Reid put sleep loss on a scale everyone understands: alcohol. In their study, people awake for 17 hours performed like they had a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05 percent. At 24 hours awake, the equivalence reached 0.10 percent, past the legal driving limit in every US state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup>

<SleepImpairment />

Milder sleep debt still costs you. Sleep-restricted people respond slower on vigilance tests and lapse more often, with some responses stretching past half a second as attention flickers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> One short night shows up in your scores the next day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup>

The fix is boring and unbeatable: seven to nine hours, on a schedule your body can predict. For the mechanisms, and what a week of poor sleep does to your scores, see our guide on <a href="/guides/how-sleep-affects-reaction-time">how sleep affects reaction time</a>.

## 3. Caffeine: one coffee does the job

Caffeine works. Across decades of studies, doses between roughly 40 and 300 mg improve reaction time, vigilance, and attention.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-7">7</a></sup> The ceiling is the interesting part.

A 2024 trial tested first-person-shooter players at two doses: 1 mg per kilogram of body weight, about 83 mg or one cup of coffee, and 3 mg per kilogram, about 250 mg. Both doses beat water on shooting accuracy and reaction time. Neither beat the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> Tripling the dose bought nothing except a higher chance of jitters, and jitters tax your accuracy.

<CaffeineZones />

Timing matters more than quantity. The esports trial measured its strongest effects 60 to 100 minutes after ingestion, so a coffee gulped right before testing peaks too late.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> Caffeine within eight hours of bed also erodes the sleep that section 2 established as your biggest lever. A morning dose is close to free; an evening dose borrows from tomorrow's scores.

Our guide on <a href="/guides/how-caffeine-affects-reaction-time">how caffeine affects reaction time</a> covers doses and timing in more depth.

## 4. Your hardware is part of your score

An online reaction score measures a chain: the screen draws the change, you perceive and react, your device registers the click, the browser stamps the time. You control more of that chain than you might think.

When researchers pointed a robot at 26 popular smartphones, total input and display delay ranged from 35 to 140 ms depending on the model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> That spread is wider than the gap between an average score and a good one, from hardware alone. Even the optimized equipment in a reaction-time lab added around 18 ms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup>

To cut the lag you can control:

- Use a wired mouse, or a gaming-grade wireless one with its own dongle. Bluetooth mice add more delay than either.
- Prefer a high refresh rate. At 60 Hz the screen redraws every 16.7 ms, so the green can appear up to a full frame late.
- Close whatever is taxing the machine. A stuttering browser stamps your click late.
- Compare scores only on the same device. A phone score and a desktop score are different measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup>

These changes leave your reflexes untouched; they stop your equipment from padding the number.

## 5. Focus beats grinding

A reaction test punishes divided attention more than almost anything else you do at a computer. The signal arrives at a random moment, and if your mind is on a notification, the milliseconds spent re-engaging go straight onto your score. Vigilance research shows the shape of the failure: long lapses scattered between clean attempts, rather than every response slowing evenly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup>

You can rig attention in your favor:

- Test in a quiet window, phone out of reach, other tabs closed.
- Breathe between attempts instead of re-clicking the moment the result lands. Rushed attempts produce false starts.
- Stop at the first sign of mental fatigue. Five focused attempts beat twenty distracted ones.

Your first session after a break is often your best. That is attention at work, and you can protect it on purpose.

## 6. Warm up your body

Exercise speeds up cognition, and the effect is acute: it appears right after a single session. A meta-analysis covering 113 studies and 4,390 participants found that one bout of exercise shortened reaction times on cognitive tasks at no cost to accuracy, with cycling and high-intensity intervals showing the largest effects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-10">10</a></sup>

The practical version: two minutes of jumping jacks, a brisk walk, or a set of air squats before a session is a legitimate performance intervention, and the cheapest one on this list. Regular training helps too, through fitness and better sleep, but the acute benefit is yours today.

## What to expect, and when

Honest numbers, based on the research above:

- **Today:** the handicap removals. Sleep, caffeine timing, a wired mouse, and a warm-up each claw back milliseconds the moment you apply them.
- **Within days:** the practice effect. Your brain tunes to the task and scores drop in the first sessions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> Part of that is skill at the test rather than raw speed, and it still counts, because everyone you compare against is measured on the same kind of test.
- **Over weeks:** trained speed. The action-game research shows response speed rising with regular fast-paced play, and the gains transfer to tasks you never trained.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

Stacked together, the difference between a sleep-deprived, distracted session on a laggy setup and a rested, caffeinated, focused one on clean hardware often spans a full rank band. Measure both ends and you will know exactly what your habits are worth.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 200 ms a good reaction time?

Yes. In a browser test, a 200 to 249 ms average lands in ReflexLab's Advanced band, ahead of typical online medians around 273 ms. Anything under 200 ms in a browser is fast, and at that level some of the difference between scores comes down to hardware rather than reflexes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup>

### Do gamers have faster reaction times?

On average, yes. Players of fast-paced action games respond faster than non-players across a range of lab tasks with no loss of accuracy, and training studies show the games cause part of that gap rather than only attracting people who were already quick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

### Why is my reaction time worse on my phone?

Phone screens and touch sensors add between 35 and 140 ms of delay depending on the model, and that delay lands in your measured score.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> Your reflexes are the same on both devices; the measuring instrument differs.
