<section id="what-is" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is an F1 reaction time test?</h2>

      An F1 reaction time test copies the start of a Grand Prix. Five red lights come on one
      second apart. They hold. They go out. The moment they go dark you click, and ReflexLab
      reports the gap in milliseconds.

      The sequence follows the
      <a href="https://api.fia.com/system/files/documents/fia_2026_f1_regulations_-_section_b_sporting_-_iss_07_-_2026-06-25.pdf" rel="noopener">FIA sporting regulations</a>:
      one second between each light, then lights out at the starter's discretion.
      <a href="https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article.rapid-decisions-driver-skills-and-intricate-procedures-why-race-starts-are.3uziERC5iPE5EYOPRmerXU.html" rel="noopener">F1.com</a>
      puts the hold at a random 0.2 to 3 seconds, and that is the window this test uses.

      This is an independent practice tool with no official standing. It copies the sequence
      because the sequence is the point: the fixed four-second ramp invites you to guess, and the
      random hold punishes the guess. If you want the plain version with no ramp, take the
      <a href="/">reaction time test</a>.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="how-it-works" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>How this F1 reaction time test works</h2>
    <ol class="steps-list section-content">
      <li>
        <strong>Choose your settings</strong>

          Pick how many starts make a session (1, 3, 5, or 10) and whether you react with a mouse
          click or the spacebar. Your choices save automatically.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Start the sequence</strong>

          Click the test area or press Space. The first light comes on at once, then one more every
          second until all five are lit.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Hold</strong>

          After the fifth light, nothing happens for somewhere between 0.2 and 3 seconds. Keep
          still. Any input while a light is lit is a jump start.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Lights out</strong>

          All five go dark at once. Click or hit Space as fast as you can. The timer runs from the
          lights going out to your input.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Review your session</strong>

          After the last start you see every time, your session average, and whether you set a new
          best. Milestones and your trend graph update below the test.

      </li>
    </ol>
    <div class="note">
      <strong>Jump starts:</strong> moving before the lights go out voids that start and you run it
      again. The session summary counts how many you made. There is no other penalty, which is
      more lenient than the stewards.
    </div>

  </div>
</section>

<section id="good-score" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is a good F1 reaction time?</h2>

      Nobody has measured a big group of people on a lights-out test like this one, so we won't
      invent an average. We can still give you a scale.

      In the lab, people react to a light coming on in about 231 milliseconds
      (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00131/full" rel="noopener">Woods et al., 2015</a>).
      A light going out is a slower cue: the one study that checked found a gap of roughly 30 to
      70 milliseconds
      (<a href="https://www.journals.vu.lt/psichologija/article/view/2623" rel="noopener">Sokolova et al., 2013</a>).
      Your screen and mouse then add a few milliseconds of their own.

      Put that together and your number here will sit above your score on the
      <a href="/">plain reaction time test</a>, which is normal.

      The milestones below are built on those figures, from a clean start under 400 milliseconds
      to driver territory and beyond. Each one takes real improvement to clear. They measure your
      progress rather than ranking you against everyone else.

<section id="driver-times" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>How fast are F1 drivers off the line?</h2>

      F1.com says drivers launch about 0.2 seconds after the lights go out. Jon Malvern, a
      performance coach at McLaren, puts a typical start reaction
      <a href="https://www.mclaren.com/racing/team/carlos-sainz-lando-norris-reaction-time/" rel="noopener">between 0.2 and 0.3 seconds</a>.
      The fastest widely reported start is Valtteri Bottas at the 2017 Austrian Grand Prix: 0.201
      seconds from lights out to the car moving, a figure the FIA measured after rivals suspected
      a jump start
      (<a href="https://www.motorsportmagazine.com/articles/single-seaters/f1/2017-austrian-grand-prix/" rel="noopener">Motor Sport Magazine</a>).

      Those numbers measure something different from a click. The reaction time shown on race
      broadcasts runs from lights out to the driver dropping the clutch paddle to its bite point,
      according to Rob Smedley, who led the system's development for F1. The 2017 figure ran to the
      car moving. Both include a physical action in a car, with the driver's hands already loaded
      on the paddle. A 230 on this page and a 0.23 on the broadcast are two different clocks.
      Treat the driver figures as a reference point, not a scoreboard.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="jump-starts" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Jump starts, here and on the grid</h2>

      Here, any click or key press while a light is lit is a jump start. The attempt is voided,
      the counter ticks up, and you go again. Guessing the hold is the usual cause: with a window
      anywhere from 0.2 to 3 seconds, a guess lands early more often than it lands fast.

      On the grid the regulations are stricter. A car has to stay stationary from the third light
      until the lights go out, and a transponder on the car detects movement over a small,
      undisclosed distance. The stewards can hand out a five or ten second penalty, a drive-through,
      or a stop-and-go. The tolerance stays secret so teams cannot tune their launches to sit just
      inside it.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="tips" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>How to get a faster start</h2>
    <div class="tips-grid grid-2x2 section-content">
      <div class="tip-card">
        <span class="tip-card-icon" aria-hidden="true">
          🎯
        </span>
        <h3>Watch the lights</h3>

          The lights going dark are the signal. Fix your eyes on the gantry, not on the text below
          it, and keep your finger resting on the button.

      </div>
      <div class="tip-card">
        <span class="tip-card-icon" aria-hidden="true">
          ⏱️
        </span>
        <h3>Don't guess the hold</h3>

          The hold is random inside a 0.2 to 3 second window. A guess that lands early costs you
          the whole start. Wait for the lights and take the honest number.

      </div>
      <div class="tip-card">
        <span class="tip-card-icon" aria-hidden="true">
          🖥️
        </span>
        <h3>Cut hardware lag</h3>

          A 60 Hz screen can add up to 16 ms before you see the lights go out, and a wireless mouse
          in power-saving mode adds more. Faster hardware does not make you quicker. It stops your
          equipment padding the number.

      </div>
      <div class="tip-card">
        <span class="tip-card-icon" aria-hidden="true">
          😴
        </span>
        <h3>Get enough sleep</h3>

          <a href="/guides/how-sleep-affects-reaction-time">Poor sleep</a> slows reaction time
          more than any setting on this page can make up for. Test when you're rested so your
          sessions compare fairly.

      </div>
    </div>
