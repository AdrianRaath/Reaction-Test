<section id="what-is" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is a visual memory test?</h2>

      A visual memory test measures how much visual information you can hold in mind at once. This
      one shows a grid of squares, lights a few of them for two seconds, then goes dark and asks you
      to click the ones you saw.

      Clear the level and it adds another tile. Level 1 asks for three squares, which feels like
      nothing. Level 10 asks for twelve, and by then you can feel the pattern slipping as you click.
      That point where it slips is your score.

      The test has no clock during recall and no ranks. It has one number: the level you reached.
      Most people land between 8 and 12, and nearly everyone improves over their first few runs.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="how-it-works" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>How this visual memory test works</h2>
    <ol class="steps-list section-content">
      <li>
        <strong>Watch the pattern</strong>

          A set of squares lights up white for two seconds. Take in the whole grid rather than
          scanning square by square.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Click them back</strong>

          The pattern goes dark and the grid border turns green. Click every square that lit up.
          Correct squares confirm green and stay lit, so you can see what you have left.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Spend your lives carefully</strong>

          You get three lives for the entire run. A wrong click costs one and flashes red, but the
          level keeps going, so you can survive a bad guess and still clear the level.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Climb until it breaks</strong>

          Each level adds a tile, and the grid grows from 3 by 3 up to 7 by 7 to make room. Your
          score is the last level you finished.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Track your progress</strong>

          Your best level, average, trend graph, milestones, and run history save in your browser,
          so you can see recall improve run over run.

      </li>
    </ol>
    <div class="note">
      <strong>Note:</strong> There is no timer during recall. Take as long as you need. The two
      seconds of viewing time is the only clock in the test.
    </div>

  </div>
</section>

<section id="what-it-measures" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What visual memory actually measures</h2>

      What this test probes is visual working memory: the short-term store that holds what you have
      just seen while you do something with it. It is small, it fades within seconds, and it is
      doing quiet work all day. You use it when you glance at a road sign and act on it a moment
      later, or when you look up from a recipe and still know where the next step sits on the page.

      The limit is real and it is lower than most people expect. Research on visual working memory
      generally puts the capacity at around three to four separate items, not the twelve squares
      level 10 asks for. Getting past that ceiling means bundling tiles into fewer, bigger units,
      which is exactly the skill this test rewards.

      Practice at the format lifts your score faster than anything else, and that is worth naming
      honestly: a high level here means you have got good at this grid, not that your memory is
      better than someone else's in general.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="tips" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>How to improve your visual memory score</h2>
    <div class="factors-list section-content">
      <div class="factor">
        <h3>Chunk tiles into shapes</h3>

          This is the whole game. Four scattered squares are four things to remember; four squares
          that form an L is one thing. Hunt for lines, corners, and clusters, and you will hold far
          more than your raw capacity allows.

      </div>
      <div class="factor">
        <h3>Look at the grid, not the squares</h3>

          Scanning tile by tile burns your two seconds and gives you a list to memorise. Softening
          your focus and taking the whole grid in at once gives you an image instead.

      </div>
      <div class="factor">
        <h3>Don't rush your first click</h3>

          Recall is untimed, so there is no reward for speed and a real cost to a wrong guess.
          Settle the picture in your head before you touch anything.

      </div>
      <div class="factor">
        <h3>Click the certain ones first</h3>

          Every correct square stays lit, which narrows what is left. Starting with the tiles you
          are sure of turns a fuzzy memory into a smaller, easier problem.

      </div>
      <div class="factor">
        <h3>Use the empty space</h3>

          Sometimes the gaps are easier to see than the tiles, especially on a dense grid. If a
          quadrant was completely dark, that is one fact worth as much as three tiles.

      </div>
      <div class="factor">
        <h3>Test when you are rested</h3>

          Working memory is one of the first things sleep loss takes. A tired run can cost two or
          three levels, which is worth knowing before you read anything into a bad score.

      </div>
    </div>

      Speed tests reward trained reflexes; this one rewards a strategy. If you want to compare the
      two, try the <a href="/">reaction time test</a> and see which feels more trainable to you.

  </div>
