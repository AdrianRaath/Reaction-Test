<section id="what-is" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is an audio reaction time test?</h2>

      An audio reaction time test measures how fast you respond to a sound. You wait for a beep
      at a random moment, then click. ReflexLab reports the gap in milliseconds.

      Most reaction tests use a light, like the green flash on the
      <a href="/">visual reaction time test</a>. Here nothing on screen changes when the beep
      plays, so you react by ear. Sprinters do the same off the starting gun, and gamers do it
      when they hear footsteps before they see anyone.

      Computers play sound late. Your browser passes the beep to the operating system, which holds
      it in a buffer before your speakers play it, and Bluetooth headphones add more delay on top.
      This test subtracts the delay your browser reports, so your time starts when the sound
      reaches you.

  </div>
</section>

    <h2>How this audio reaction time test works</h2>
    <ol class="steps-list section-content">
      <li>
        <strong>Choose your settings</strong>

          Pick how many beeps make a session (1, 3, 5, or 10), how long the random wait can run,
          and whether you answer with a mouse click or the spacebar. The page remembers your
          choices.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Run a sound check</strong>

          Press Sound check in the toolbar to hear the test beep once, and set a volume that's
          easy to hear. The check also shows how much audio delay your device adds.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Listen</strong>

          Click the test area or press Space. The box turns red and stays red, even after the beep
          plays, so the sound is your only cue.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>React</strong>

          Click or hit Space the moment you hear the beep. The timer runs from the moment the
          sound leaves your speakers until you respond.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Review your session</strong>

          After the last beep you see each time, your average, your rank, and the audio delay the
          test subtracted. Your personal best, ranks, and trend graph update below the test.

      </li>
    </ol>
    <div class="note">
      <strong>False starts and misses:</strong> a response before the beep, or within 100 ms of
      it, is a false start, and you run that round again. If you don't respond within three
      seconds of the beep, the round doesn't count. The usual cause is a muted or low volume.
    </div>

</section>

<section id="good-score" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is a good audio reaction time?</h2>

      A good audio reaction time is under 210 ms. In the largest study we found, 7,081 Swedish
      adults aged 27 to 54 took a beep test on their own computers. After the researchers
      corrected for differences between computer setups, the group averaged 241 ms
      (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00407/full" rel="noopener">Madison et al., 2016</a>),
      and most people landed within about 50 ms of that.

      Trained sprinters react faster. At world championships from 2003 to 2009, men in the 100 m
      left the blocks an average of 166 ms after the gun, and finalists averaged 142 ms in the
      final
      (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22739331/" rel="noopener">Tønnessen et al., 2013</a>).
      We built the ranks below from those numbers:

<section id="auditory-vs-visual" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Auditory vs visual reaction time</h2>

      In the lab, people react to sound faster than to light. Jain and colleagues tested 120
      medical students on the same laptop and measured 228 ms for a beep against 248 ms for a red
      circle
      (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4456887/" rel="noopener">Jain et al., 2015</a>).
      Shelton and Kumar found a wider gap in 14 people: 284 ms for sound and 331 ms for light
      (<a href="https://www.scirp.org/html/4-2400003_2689.htm" rel="noopener">2010</a>).

      On an ordinary computer the gap can shrink or flip, because browsers play sound later than
      they update the screen. Bridges and colleagues timed online testing platforms on Windows
      and found the sound arriving 32 to 122 ms after the matching image
      (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7512138/" rel="noopener">2020</a>). A sound
      test that ignores this delay can read slower than a visual test.

      This page subtracts the audio delay your browser reports. The visual test doesn't remove
      your screen's delay, so expect your gap to come out a little wider than the lab figures. To
      measure it, take the <a href="/">visual reaction time test</a> and this one in the same
      sitting, five rounds each, with the same input method.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="audio-delay" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Headphones, speakers, and audio delay</h2>

      Your device plays sound a little late. On the Windows PC we built this test on, Chrome
      reported about 70 ms between the page scheduling the beep and the speakers playing it. Left
      in, that delay would add 70 ms to every time you set.

      Bluetooth adds much more. RTINGS measured 166 to 173 ms on standard Bluetooth codecs and 34
      ms with aptX Low Latency
      (<a href="https://www.rtings.com/headphones/learn/latency" rel="noopener">RTINGS</a>).
      That is more than the gap between an average score and an elite one.

      This test subtracts the delay your browser reports, and the results screen shows how much
      it took off. On a Mac, browsers include Bluetooth in that figure. On Windows we found no
      sign that they do, so a Bluetooth score there includes the extra delay. Sound check flags
      any delay over 100 ms as likely wireless audio. For your most accurate score, use wired
      headphones or your device speakers.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="sprint-starts" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Reacting to the starting gun</h2>

      Sprinters have a rule about how fast they can react to sound. Sensors in the starting
      blocks time each athlete from the gun, and World Athletics treats any reaction under 0.100
      seconds as a possible false start (Technical Rule 16.6 in the
      <a href="https://worldathletics.org/about-iaaf/documents/book-of-rules" rel="noopener">Book of Rules</a>).
      Outside the combined events, a sprinter who false starts is disqualified.

      Researchers still argue over the 100 ms line. Pain and Hibbs recorded athletes reacting
      in under 100 ms
      (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17127583/" rel="noopener">2007</a>). Lipps and
      colleagues studied 425 sprinters at the 2008 Olympics and put the fastest possible reaction
      at 109 ms for men and 121 ms for women
      (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198384/" rel="noopener">2011</a>).
      This test uses the same line: respond within 100 ms of the beep and the round counts as a
      false start.

      People react faster to louder sounds. When Brown and colleagues raised a recorded gunshot
      from 80 to 120 dB, sprint-start reactions dropped from 138 to 120 ms. At the 2004 Olympics,
      runners in lane 1, closest to the starter, reacted in 160 ms against 175 ms for the other
      lanes
      (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18460990/" rel="noopener">2008</a>).

  </div>
</section>
