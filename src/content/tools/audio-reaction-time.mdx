<section id="what-is" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is an audio reaction time test?</h2>

      An audio reaction time test measures how fast you respond to a sound. You wait, a beep
      plays at a random moment, and you click. ReflexLab reports the gap in milliseconds.

      Most reaction tests use a light, like the green flash on the
      <a href="/">visual reaction time test</a>. On this page the screen holds still through the
      beep, so only your ears can tell you when to go. Sprinters react to a starting gun and
      gamers react to footsteps they can't see yet, so this is a skill you already use.

      Computers play sound late. Your browser hands the beep to the operating system, which
      buffers it before your speakers move, and Bluetooth headphones add far more. This test
      reads the delay your browser reports and subtracts it, so your time starts when the sound
      reaches you.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="how-it-works" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>How this audio reaction time test works</h2>
    <ol class="steps-list section-content">
      <li>
        <strong>Choose your settings</strong>

          Pick how many beeps make a session (1, 3, 5, or 10), how long the random wait can run,
          and whether you answer with a mouse click or the spacebar. Your choices save
          automatically.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Run a sound check</strong>

          Press Sound check in the toolbar. You hear the test beep once and can set a clear,
          comfortable volume. The test also reads how much audio delay your device adds.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Listen</strong>

          Click the test area or press Space. The box turns red and stays red. After a random
          wait the beep plays, and nothing on screen changes.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>React</strong>

          Click or hit Space the moment you hear it. The timer runs from the beep reaching your
          speakers to your input.

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Review your session</strong>

          After the last beep you see every time, your average, your rank, and the audio delay
          the test subtracted. Your personal best, ranks, and trend graph update below the test.

      </li>
    </ol>
    <div class="note">
      <strong>False starts and misses:</strong> any input before the beep, or within 100 ms of
      it, is a false start, and you run that round again. If three seconds pass after the beep
      with no response, the round is void. That usually means your volume is down.
    </div>

  </div>
</section>

<section id="good-score" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>What is a good audio reaction time?</h2>

      A good audio reaction time is under 210 ms. The best large sample we found tested 7,081
      Swedish adults aged 27 to 54 on their own computers. They averaged 241 ms once the
      researchers corrected for differences between computer setups
      (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00407/full" rel="noopener">Madison et al., 2016</a>),
      and most landed within about 50 ms of that.

      Trained sprinters go faster. At world championships from 2003 to 2009, men in the 100 m
      left the blocks an average of 166 ms after the gun, and finalists averaged 142 ms in the
      final itself
      (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22739331/" rel="noopener">Tønnessen et al., 2013</a>).
      The ranks below sit on those numbers:

<section id="auditory-vs-visual" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Auditory vs visual reaction time</h2>

      In the lab, people react to sound faster than to light. Jain and colleagues tested 120
      medical students on the same laptop and measured 228 ms for a beep against 248 ms for a red
      circle
      (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4456887/" rel="noopener">Jain et al., 2015</a>).
      A smaller study of 14 people found a wider gap: 284 ms for sound, 331 ms for light
      (<a href="https://www.scirp.org/html/4-2400003_2689.htm" rel="noopener">Shelton and Kumar, 2010</a>).

      On an ordinary computer the gap can shrink or flip. Browsers play sound later than they
      draw pixels: Bridges and colleagues timed online testing platforms on Windows and found the
      sound arriving 32 to 122 ms after the matching image
      (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7512138/" rel="noopener">Bridges et al., 2020</a>). An
      uncorrected sound test can read slower than a visual one for that reason alone. This page
      subtracts the audio delay your browser reports, which gives sound a fair race. The visual test keeps your screen's delay in its number, so expect your gap
      here to run a little wider than in the lab.

      To measure your own gap, take the <a href="/">visual reaction time test</a> and this one in
      the same sitting, five rounds each, with the same input method.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="audio-delay" class="info-section">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Headphones, speakers, and audio delay</h2>

      Your device plays sound a little late. On the Windows PC we built this test on, Chrome
      reported about 70 ms between the page scheduling the beep and the speakers playing it.
      Uncorrected, that delay would land on every time you set.

      Bluetooth adds far more. RTINGS measured 166 to 173 ms on standard Bluetooth codecs and 34
      ms with aptX Low Latency
      (<a href="https://www.rtings.com/headphones/learn/latency" rel="noopener">RTINGS</a>).
      That is more than the gap between an average score and an elite one.

      This test reads the delay your browser reports and subtracts it, and the results screen
      shows how much it took off. On a Mac, browsers include Bluetooth in that figure. On Windows
      we found no sign that they do, so a Bluetooth score there carries the extra delay. Sound
      check flags anything over 100 ms as likely wireless audio. For your most accurate score,
      use wired headphones or your device speakers.

  </div>
</section>

<section id="sprint-starts" class="info-section alt-bg">
  <div class="container">
    <h2>Reacting to the starting gun</h2>

      Sprinting puts rules on auditory reaction time. Sensors in the starting blocks time each
      athlete from the gun, and World Athletics treats any reaction under 0.100 seconds as a
      possible false start (Technical Rule 16.6 in the
      <a href="https://worldathletics.org/about-iaaf/documents/book-of-rules" rel="noopener">Book of Rules</a>).
      Outside the combined events, a sprinter who false starts is disqualified.

      Researchers still argue over the 100 ms line. Pain and Hibbs recorded athletes reacting
      in under 100 ms
      (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17127583/" rel="noopener">2007</a>). Lipps and
      colleagues studied 425 sprinters at the 2008 Olympics and put the fastest possible reaction
      at 109 ms for men and 121 ms for women
      (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198384/" rel="noopener">2011</a>).
      This test borrows the line: respond within 100 ms of the beep and the round counts as a
      false start.

      Loudness changes the result. In a lab study, raising a recorded gunshot from 80 to 120 dB
      cut sprint-start reaction from 138 to 120 ms. At the 2004 Olympics, runners in lane 1,
      closest to the starter, reacted in 160 ms against 175 ms for the other lanes
      (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18460990/" rel="noopener">Brown et al., 2008</a>).

  </div>
</section>

          start out of you.
